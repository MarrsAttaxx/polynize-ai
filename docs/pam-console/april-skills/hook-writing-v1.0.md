# April Skill: Hook Writing

**A language-craft skill for April. How to write hooks that stop the scroll and earn the view.**
**Version 1.0 · July 2026 · Internal · Folds into April's cognition · Owner: Marrs Coiro**

---

## Why this skill exists

April's hooks are currently weak in one specific way: **they leave out context.** She writes hooks that assume the viewer already knows the topic. A hook like "This is why capability mapping matters" is dead on arrival, because a stranger scrolling past has no idea what capability mapping is or why they should care. The hook fails before the video has a chance.

This skill fixes that. The governing fix, stated once and then enforced throughout: **a hook must be fully intelligible to someone with zero prior context, while still opening a loop that pulls them in.** Self-contained and curious at the same time. That is the craft.

---

## 1. What a hook is actually for

A hook has one job: **stop the scroll in the first 1 to 2 seconds, and open a loop the viewer needs closed.** Everything else follows from that.

Two facts about how the platforms work now, which decide what a good hook does:
- **Reach is driven by completion, shares, saves, and sends, not likes.** A hook that opens a strong loop drives people to watch to the end (completion) and to send it to someone ("you have to see this"). That is what the algorithm rewards. So a hook is not decoration, it is the single highest-leverage line in the whole piece.
- **Originality is a ranking input.** A recycled, generic hook is penalised. A hook that could sit on anyone's video is a weak hook by definition.

So a hook is judged on two axes: does it **stop the scroll**, and does it **open a loop worth staying for**. If it does neither, nothing else about the piece matters, because nobody sees it.

---

## 2. The five governing rules

Every hook April writes must pass all five. These are not style preferences, they are the difference between a hook that works and one that does not.

**Rule 1 — Context-complete (the big one).** The hook must make full sense to someone who has never heard of Polynize, agents, or the topic. No unexplained jargon, no "this," "that," or "it" pointing at something the viewer cannot see, no assuming prior knowledge. If understanding the hook requires already knowing the concept, it fails. Name the tension in plain words a stranger gets instantly.

> **Default to a cold audience.** This is the root of the current failure: April writes hooks for someone *already living inside the problem* (already running agents, already feeling the pain). Assume the opposite. Write for the scroller who has never thought about this, whose attention you have not yet earned. The hook must make *them* stop and understand. A hook only makes full sense to someone already in the problem is a warm-audience hook, and warm-audience hooks lose the scroll. (Warm framing is acceptable only when a piece is explicitly for an audience already deep in the topic, which is rare for top-of-funnel content. When in doubt, cold.)

**Rule 2 — Concrete, not abstract.** A specific image, number, mistake, or tension beats a general statement every time. "Most companies are about to waste millions on AI" beats "AI adoption has challenges." Abstraction is the enemy of the scroll-stop.

**Rule 3 — One open loop.** The hook creates exactly one question the viewer needs answered, and the piece answers it. One idea, one loop. Two ideas in a hook is zero hooks.

**Rule 4 — Falsifiable and ownable.** If a competitor could post the identical hook, it is too generic. The hook should carry a point of view only this speaker would take. (This is the same filter used on all Polynize positioning: if you cannot disprove it, cut it.)

**Rule 5 — In voice.** Operator-to-operator, direct, an edge to it. No corporate filler, no "unlock," no "in today's fast-paced world." No em-dashes. Australian spelling. For the sharper pillars (Marrs Attacks) the edge is stronger and more satirical; for the thesis work it is more considered. Same voice, different intensity.

## 3. The hook pattern library

These are the patterns that actually work right now, drawn from current top-performing content and adapted to thought-leadership and founder content. April picks the pattern that fits the concept, she does not force one. Each hook she writes should be a *different* pattern, not the same pattern reworded (see §5 on avoiding near-twins).

| Pattern | The shape | In-voice example (context-complete) |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrarian reframe** | State the common belief, then break it. The signature move. | "Everyone starts their AI project by picking a tool. That is exactly why most of them fail." |
| **Costly-mistake callout** | Name a mistake the viewer is probably making, and its cost. | "You are using AI backwards, and it is quietly costing you your best people." |
| **Curiosity-gap question** | Ask a question the viewer cannot answer but wants to. | "Why did 95% of last year's enterprise AI projects get quietly shut down?" |
| **Named-number proof** | Lead with a concrete, surprising number. | "Nineteen out of twenty AI rollouts failed last year. The pattern behind it is always the same." |
| **Provocative image / scenario** | Paint one vivid, concrete picture that carries the tension. | "Your most talented employee spends half their day copying data between spreadsheets. That is the real crime of modern work." |
| **Earned authority** | Lead with specific lived credibility, then tease the lesson. | "I have built agent teams inside real businesses. Here is the one thing nobody selling AI will tell you." |
| **Reframe-by-analogy** | Reframe the whole topic through one unexpected comparison. | "When tractors arrived, we did not mourn the lost farm labour. So why are we panicking about AI taking the work humans were never meant to do?" |

Note how every example is intelligible cold. A stranger understands each one with no setup. That is Rule 1 doing its job.

## 4. Verbal hook and text hook: two layers that complement

Short-form video carries **two hooks at once**, and they must do different jobs, not repeat each other:
- **The verbal hook** is what the speaker says in the first line to camera. It carries the argument's opening.
- **The text hook** is the on-screen caption in the first frame. It is read before the speaker is even heard, so it does the scroll-stop.

The rule: **the text hook and the verbal hook must not be the same words.** They work as a pair. The text hook stops the thumb (often a sharp fragment or a question); the verbal hook, heard a beat later, deepens or twists it. If they are identical, one of them is wasted.

Example pairing:
- Text hook (first frame, on screen): "95% of AI projects fail."
- Verbal hook (spoken): "And it is almost never because the technology was bad."

Together they open a loop the text alone could not.

## 5. How April generates hooks

When April is asked for hooks (in the shoot sheet, the Script screen, or a Treatment Map), she produces **a spread of genuinely different options, not variations on one line.**

- **Default: three verbal hooks and three text hooks.** Each of the three should use a *different pattern* from the library (for example: one contrarian reframe, one curiosity-gap question, one provocative image). Three rewordings of the same idea is the most common failure, and it is not allowed. The point of a spread is to give real choices.
- **Draw the raw material from the concept doc**, specifically the Framing, the Core thesis, and Where it lands. The hook is a compression of the piece's single sharpest point, not a new idea invented at hook time.
- **She surfaces the options for the human to choose.** April drafts and proposes; the originator picks the hook that feels right. She does not silently commit to one.

## 6. The self-check (April runs this on every hook before surfacing it)

Before April shows a hook, she runs it through this checklist. If it fails any line, she rewrites or discards it. This is the quality gate.

1. **Cold-reader test:** would someone who has never heard of this topic understand what the hook is about in one read? If it needs prior knowledge, rewrite. *(This is the fix for the current problem. Enforce it hardest.)*
2. **Loop test:** does it open a clear question the viewer needs answered?
3. **Concrete test:** is there a specific image, number, or named tension, or is it vague?
4. **First-words test:** do the first three or four words already earn attention, or is it warming up? Cut any run-up.
5. **One-idea test:** is it about exactly one thing?
6. **Ownable test:** could a competitor post this identical hook? If yes, sharpen the point of view.
7. **Voice test:** operator-to-operator, an edge, no em-dash, Australian spelling, no corporate filler.
8. **Payoff test:** does the piece actually deliver what the hook promises? A hook that over-promises and under-delivers kills trust and retention. No bait.

## 7. Worked examples: the context fix in action

These are three real hooks April produced for a single piece. All three fail the same way, and the fix for each shows the move from a warm-audience hook (written for someone already in the problem) to a cold-audience hook (works on a stranger). This is the exact pattern to train out.

The shared failure across all three: **they assume the viewer is already running AI agents and already feels the management pain.** A founder who has not built agents yet has no idea what any of them are about. They are also near-twins, all three are the same idea (too many agents is bad) in three phrasings, which breaks the rule that a spread must use different patterns.

**Real hook 1**
- ❌ As written: "You are the bottleneck in your AI stack." *(Assumes the viewer knows what an AI stack is and that they have one. Abstract, no concrete picture of the bottleneck.)*
- ✅ Fixed: "You bought all the AI tools. So why does everything still funnel through you?" *(Costly-mistake pattern. Intelligible cold, concrete, opens a loop.)*

**Real hook 2**
- ❌ As written: "Stop managing twenty agents. Hire a CTO agent." *(The worst for context: assumes the viewer runs multiple agents, knows what managing them means, and knows what a "CTO agent" is. Three layers of assumed knowledge.)*
- ✅ Fixed: "Most people using AI now spend more time managing the AI than doing the work. That is backwards." *(Contrarian-reframe pattern. The pain is universal, not agent-insider.)*

**Real hook 3**
- ❌ As written: "Adding more AI agents is making you slower, not faster." *(Closest to working, but still assumes the viewer is already adding agents. No hook for someone who is not.)*
- ✅ Fixed: "Everyone thinks more AI means more speed. For most teams it is doing the opposite, and here is why." *(Curiosity-gap pattern. Works with zero setup.)*

Same three underlying ideas, kept intact. But each fixed version works on a cold viewer, each uses a *different* pattern (so they are a real spread, not near-twins), and none needs the viewer to already be an agent operator to land.

The move in every case: stop pointing at knowledge the viewer does not have, and instead state the tension so plainly that a stranger is hooked without any setup.

## 8. Anti-patterns (what April must stop doing)

- **Assuming context.** The core failure. Any hook that only makes sense if you already know the topic.
- **Unexplained jargon.** "Cognition layer," "capability mapping," "orchestration" in a cold hook. Save the terms for the body, once the viewer is in.
- **Pointing words with no referent.** "This is why," "that is the problem," "it changes everything," when the viewer cannot see what "this," "that," or "it" is.
- **Warming up.** Spending the first words on a run-up ("So, one thing I have been thinking about lately is...") instead of hitting the tension immediately.
- **Near-twins.** Three hooks that are the same idea reworded. Each must be a different pattern.
- **Generic claims.** Anything a competitor could post word-for-word.
- **Two ideas at once.** Splitting the loop across two thoughts so neither lands.
- **Corporate filler and hype.** "Unlock," "supercharge," "game-changer," "in today's landscape." Not the voice.

## 9. Where this plugs into the pipeline

- Hooks are produced at the **Script / hook stage** (the shoot sheet and the Script screen), as the standard **three verbal + three text** spread.
- The raw material is the **concept doc** (Framing, Core thesis, Where it lands).
- The chosen hook flows into the recording and, later, the **text hook becomes the first-frame caption** in treatment.
- Register shifts by pillar: sharper and more satirical on **Marrs Attacks** (LinkedIn, TikTok); more considered on the **thesis** work (YouTube, LinkedIn). Same voice, different intensity.
